📊 Foundation Concepts

Consumer Price Index &
Inflation — A Deep Dive

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is the primary benchmark used by the Government of India and the Reserve Bank of India to measure the rate of change in prices of a representative basket of goods and services consumed by households. Understanding CPI is essential for grasping how inflation affects purchasing power, monetary policy, and economic well-being.

🧺

What is CPI?

CPI measures the weighted average price of a fixed basket of consumer goods across urban and rural households. India's CPI is compiled by the Ministry of Statistics & Programme Implementation (MoSPI) and tracks ~299 items across 6 major sub-groups. Base year is currently 2012 = 100.

CPI = (Cost of Basket in Current Year ÷ Cost of Basket in Base Year) × 100
📈

Inflation Rate

Inflation is the rate of change in CPI over a period — typically measured year-on-year (YoY). When CPI rises from 160 to 168 in a year, the inflation rate is 5%. This means ₹100 worth of goods last year now costs ₹105, reducing your real purchasing power by 5%.

Inflation (%) = [(CPIcurrent − CPIprevious) ÷ CPIprevious] × 100
🏦

RBI's Monetary Policy

Under the Inflation Targeting Framework (adopted in 2016), the RBI Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) is legally mandated to maintain CPI inflation at 4% ± 2%. If inflation exceeds 6% for three consecutive quarters, RBI must submit a written explanation to the government — a major accountability mechanism.

Target: 4% (Band: 2%–6%) | Mandate: RBI Act, 1934 (Amended 2016)
⚖️

CPI vs WPI: Key Difference

CPI measures prices at the retail/consumer level — what you pay at a shop. WPI (Wholesale Price Index) measures prices at the producer/wholesale level — factory gates and warehouses. CPI reflects consumer burden; WPI reflects production cost pressures. India uses CPI (Combined) as the primary inflation benchmark for monetary policy since 2014.

CPI → Consumer end | WPI → Producer end | GDP Deflator → Economy-wide

Types of Inflation — An Overview

Demand-Pull

Demand-Pull Inflation

Occurs when aggregate demand in an economy exceeds aggregate supply. Classic scenario: government spending surges, consumers have more money, but production hasn't scaled — "too much money chasing too few goods." Common during economic booms.

Example: Post-COVID spending surge (2021–22)
Cost-Push

Cost-Push Inflation

Triggered when production costs rise — raw materials, energy, wages — forcing producers to raise prices. India frequently experiences this via oil price shocks and monsoon failures affecting food supply. Not easily controlled by monetary policy alone.

Example: Crude oil shock → fuel → freight → food prices
Built-In

Built-In (Wage-Price) Inflation

A self-fulfilling cycle: workers expect inflation and demand higher wages; higher wages increase production costs; businesses raise prices to compensate; repeat. Also called "wage-price spiral." Hardest to break without significant monetary tightening.

Example: Inflationary expectations entrenched in wage negotiations
Structural

Structural Inflation

Arises from structural deficiencies in an economy — poor supply chains, inadequate storage, land fragmentation, regulatory bottlenecks. Particularly relevant in India's agriculture sector where post-harvest losses can exceed 25%, creating artificial scarcity.

Example: Onion/tomato price spikes due to supply chain gaps

How the calculation works: Each category has an official weight (e.g., Food = 45.86%). Your input price is divided by the base price (₹100) to get a price ratio. This ratio is then multiplied by the category weight. The sum of all weighted ratios × 100 gives the CPI. Inflation = (CPI − 100) / 100 × 100%. This mirrors the exact methodology used by MoSPI.
⛽ Case Study

Fuel Price Hike:
The Macroeconomic Chain Reaction

A rise in petroleum prices is one of the most potent triggers of cost-push inflation in a developing economy like India. Because fuel is an input cost across virtually every sector — agriculture, manufacturing, logistics, and services — its price transmission effect is both swift and wide-ranging.

India's Oil Import Dependency ~85%
Crude Oil in Import Bill ~22%
Fuel Weight in CPI 6.84%
Indirect Fuel Impact on CPI ~30%+
⛽
Stage 1 — Origin

International Crude Oil Price Rise

India imports ~85% of its crude oil needs. When Brent crude rises on global markets (due to OPEC+ cuts, geopolitical conflict, or USD appreciation), the cost of India's oil import bill increases sharply. India is a price-taker — it cannot influence global crude prices. The rupee's depreciation further amplifies the cost since crude is priced in USD.

Brent Crude Impact: Every $10/barrel increase ≈ ₹1 lakh crore added to India's import bill
🏭
Stage 2 — Refinery & Distribution

Cost Absorption by OMCs / Retail Price Revision

Oil Marketing Companies (OMCs) — Indian Oil, BPCL, HPCL — either absorb losses (leading to "under-recoveries") or pass costs to consumers via retail price revisions. The government may partially cushion the impact through excise duty cuts or subsidies, but this creates a fiscal burden. When prices are revised upward, it directly enters the CPI's "Fuel & Light" and transport sub-indices.

Petrol: ₹72/L (2020) → ₹106/L (2022) — a 47% increase over 2 years
🚛
Stage 3 — Logistics & Supply Chain

Transportation Cost Surge — Freight Rate Hike

Diesel powers >95% of India's freight movement. A diesel price hike immediately raises the cost per kilometre for road transport. Transporters pass this cost onto shippers as higher freight rates. This cost cascades through the supply chain — manufacturers, wholesalers, and retailers each add a margin, causing a multiplier effect on final product prices.

A 10% diesel hike → ~3–5% increase in road freight rates (IFFCO-Tokio estimates)
🛒
Stage 4 — Consumer Market

Retail Price Escalation — Broad-Based Inflation

Higher transport costs feed into prices of food, FMCG, electronics, and construction materials. Since food has the highest CPI weight (45.86%), even a modest rise in agricultural commodity transport costs causes notable CPI movement. This is "core CPI inflation" being driven by supply-side pressure — a textbook case of cost-push inflation.

Food inflation rose to 8.7% in Dec 2023 — partly fuel-driven freight costs
🏦
Stage 5 — Policy Response

RBI's Monetary Tightening — Repo Rate Hike

When inflation breaches the upper tolerance band of 6%, the RBI's MPC votes to raise the Repo Rate — the rate at which it lends to commercial banks. A higher Repo Rate makes borrowing expensive: home loans, business credit, and consumer credit all become costlier. This suppresses demand, reducing inflationary pressure over time. However, this also slows GDP growth — a classic policy trade-off.

RBI raised Repo Rate by 250 bps (2.5%) between May 2022 and Feb 2023

💰 Fiscal Policy

Union Budget of India:
Structure & Significance

The Union Budget, presented annually on February 1 by the Finance Minister, is the government's comprehensive financial statement — detailing its income, expenditure plans, and economic priorities for the coming fiscal year (April 1 to March 31). It is both a policy document and a legal mandate.

📥

Revenue Budget

Day-to-day income & expenses

The Revenue Budget covers transactions that do not affect the government's asset or liability position. It includes Revenue Receipts (taxes, dividends, interest) and Revenue Expenditure (salaries, pensions, subsidies, interest payments). A surplus here is ideal; a deficit adds to borrowings.

Capital Budget

Long-term assets & investments

The Capital Budget deals with transactions affecting the government's assets and liabilities. Capital Receipts include borrowings and disinvestment. Capital Expenditure (Capex) goes toward infrastructure — roads, railways, defence equipment, and hospitals. Higher Capex creates a multiplier effect on GDP.

Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003

The FRBM Act mandates fiscal discipline by setting targets for reducing fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP and eliminating the revenue deficit. It requires the government to present a Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement and a Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement alongside the budget. The NK Singh Committee (2017) recommended a "Fiscal Council" to monitor compliance independently. During crises (COVID-19), the "escape clause" (Section 4(3)) allows temporary deviation from targets.
